Project v2 plan

v1 takes plain words and returns one recipe, plus the evidence for why that one. v2 builds on that: the directions you actually bake from, the ingredients you already have, and the recipes you’ve kept.

Most of what follows is about the screen rather than the scoring: what a recommendation shows you, what you are able to ask it for, and whether the numbers underneath it are worth trusting.

Where v1 landed

The Perfect Batch exists because searching up several cookie recipes and deciding which measurements to trust is overstimulating. v1 removes the deciding: you type what you want and get back a single recipe with the reasons behind it. The box takes plain words, like chewy, oat, or extra crispy peanut butter, or quick, no mixer.

325recipes, harvested automatically from schema.org Recipe JSON-LD. No manual entry.
94%of ingredient lines convert to grams, 3,396 of 3,632. This is what makes recipes comparable at all.
per g flourevery quantity is expressed per gram of flour, so batch size divides out.
132automated tests, on Next.js 16, Supabase with row-level security, Clerk and Vercel.

The corpus is five dish types: chocolate chip 157, oatmeal 73, peanut butter 55, sugar 30, snickerdoodle 10. The core task stays behind sign-in.

v1's honesty loop. A search box that says what it failed to understand is more trustworthy than one that silently guesses. Most of what follows is an extension of that principle rather than a departure from it.

What I noticed about v1 that can be improved

Four things stood out from using it myself. The plan below is ordered by them.

I often don’t have the ingredients

A recipe I can’t make tonight isn’t really a recommendation. v1 asks what texture I want and never asks what is in the cupboard.

I want to see the directions, not just the numbers

The evidence for why this one is the strongest part of v1. The method is still a wall of text on someone else’s site, which is where I actually have to go to bake.

Some words don’t parse, and I expected more accurate results

The box says what it failed to understand, which is honest, but being told a word was ignored is not the same as being understood. A few of the numbers behind the ranking are off too.

Saving doesn’t go anywhere yet

I can save a recipe and the list remembers what I searched for, but nothing uses it. Right now it is a bookmark, not a signal.

The plan

Fix first

the two things I noticed most

Recipe directions, shown visually

The piece I most want to build. A TA recommended an example I want to take inspiration from: a recipe treated as a structured object you read at a glance rather than a paragraph of steps. Quantities, order of operations, and the points where timing matters become things you find by position instead of by reading.

Design problem: it still has to carry v1’s evidence, the plots and the reasons and the runners-up, without turning into a dashboard.

More words understood, and more accurate numbers behind them

Widen the vocabulary, and rank away from a texture rather than only toward one. “Not cakey” is currently dropped and reported, which is a dead end for a reasonable request.

The accuracy half is two known faults. A few recipes in the corpus are not really the dish they were filed under, which widens every range they sit in. And oats are not counted as flour, so oatmeal recipes read as fattier than they are.

This is a design problem before it is an arithmetic one: the plots present a wrong number with exactly the same confidence as a right one, so the interface is quietly vouching for something it has not earned.

Design problem: oats do some of flour’s structural work but develop no gluten, so how much to count is a judgement call, and whatever I choose changes the denominator of every number on screen. It has to be stated in the interface, not buried in the scoring.

Add next

new capability, once the numbers are trustworthy

Tell it what you have

Enter the ingredients in the cupboard and get back what is actually makeable. This inverts the question v1 asks, so it needs to be a filter on candidates rather than another thing to type into the same box.

Design problem: deciding what counts as missing. No flour is disqualifying; no vanilla is not. Structural ingredients are requirements, flavourings are preferences, and the interface has to make that distinction visible rather than silently forgiving.

Recommendations from what you’ve saved

Saves already store the words that were typed when the recipe was kept, so the signal exists and is unused. Give Save a purpose: the saved set becomes a starting point for the next recommendation.

Design problem: the reasons panel has to say that a pick came from the saved set, or the app starts making decisions it cannot explain, which is the opposite of what v1 is for.

A movable texture control

A slider for chewy-to-crispy, so a result can be nudged without retyping. The free-text box still wins the front door. It is the thing that makes this app itself, and two equal ways in would weaken both. The slider belongs after the answer, as an adjustment.

Design problem: its range has to be the real spread of the candidates, not 0 to 100, or it promises precision the corpus cannot supply.

Other baked goods

Brownies, muffins, banana bread. The harvesting already generalises; the scoring does not.

Design problem: per gram of flour stops being comparable across categories. A brownie is not a cookie with different numbers. Comparison pools have to become per-category, and the interface has to show which pool you are looking at. A dot plot that silently changed what it was comparing would be worse than no plot.

Potential

further out

Live search beyond the corpus

Searching the whole web at request time instead of a fixed set of 325.

What it would cost: every number in this app rests on 94% of ingredient lines converting to grams, measured over a corpus I harvested and checked. Trusting an arbitrary page’s structured data at request time gives that up, and a recommendation with unverified measurements behind it is worse than a smaller library.
